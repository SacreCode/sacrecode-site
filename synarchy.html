Fig. 01The defect in the pipeline

The merge is where the thinking _dies

Every major agent pipeline ends the same way. Specialists fan out, judgments come back, and an aggregation step reconciles them into one answer before any human sees them. The topologies differ. The terminal operation does not.

That compression step is treated as the point of orchestration. I think it is the defect. The merge destroys exactly the information a decision-maker needs: which agent disagreed, about what, how severely, and what none of them covered at all. Salesforce's own research on multi-agent systems documents the failure and calls it echoing. Agents converge on agreeable output because the architecture rewards agreement.

The user receives a smooth answer to a rough reality, and has no way to see the roughness.

This is usually framed as a quality problem, something better prompting will fix. It is a structural problem. As long as the pipeline terminates in synthesis, the system decides what the human gets to deliberate about. There is a name for that arrangement, and it was written down in 1922.

Fig. 03A 1922 law, read as engineering

A law that describes a _2026 bug

Vladimir Shmakov, a Russian engineer and philosopher, published The Law of Synarchy in 1922. Strip the esoteric vocabulary and two claims remain, and both of them are engineering claims.

syn·archein

Greek · together + to rule

Rule exercised jointly. Order that does not require absorption into one centre.

mon·as

Greek · a unit

Shmakov's name for whatever holds a field of elements in ordered relation without flattening them.

First: reality is organised as a dual hierarchy. Organising centres on one axis, the fields they organise on the other. The rank of a centre is measured by one thing only: how much heterogeneity it can hold in order while the parts keep their individuality.

A centre that produces order by flattening its elements into sameness is not higher. It is degenerate.

Second: hierarchies can invert. When a lower centre expands upward and starts organising the level above it, the subject becomes an element in its own tool's field. Shmakov treated this as the structural root of tyranny. In orchestration terms the inversion is precise. The moment a coordination layer pre-decides what the human deliberates about, the human stops being the organising centre of the decision and becomes input to the system's output.

Read this way, the merge-terminated pipeline violates both claims at once. It produces order by suppressing difference, and it relocates synthesis from the person to the infrastructure.

Fig. 04The substitution

Six commitments, one removed _operation

Synarchic orchestration keeps the familiar infrastructure and removes one operation: the fan-in merge. Everything else follows from that removal.

  1. Sovereign agents

    Each agent reasons from one bounded perspective: strategic outcomes, behavioural patterns, affective signals, counter-hypotheses. Domains do not overlap, and agents do not see each other's outputs during generation. Parallel fan-out, no cross-talk, no shared scratchpad.

  2. Cartography, not aggregation

    A dedicated layer reads all outputs after generation. Its job is to map relations between them, never to reconcile. Every intersection is typed: agreement, opposition, crossed stakes, one-sided coverage, shared silence.

  3. The map is the deliverable

    The output is a structured disagreement, attributable claim by claim. There is no final answer field in the schema.

  4. Weights live with the human

    If a composite score exists, it is computed in the interface from sliders the person controls, and it is never stored server-side. Move a slider and the score moves.

  5. Sign-off is a protocol state

    The workflow terminates in an input-required state addressed to a named person. The run is structurally incomplete until a human closes it. Accountability stops diffusing because the protocol will not let it diffuse.

  6. The orchestrator holds no view

    It schedules, routes, and enforces the schema. The moment it starts interpreting, it has colonised a domain that is not its own, and the architecture treats that as a fault.

The fifth relation type matters most. Shared silence, the question no perspective addressed, is the system's own blind spot made visible. A merge architecture cannot produce it at all, because its output format is a single position.

Agreement

Two perspectives land in the same place for their own reasons. Convergence that was earned, not engineered.

Opposition

Direct contradiction inside one question. The decision's real fault line, kept visible and attributable.

Crossed stakes

Conflict across domains. What one view prizes is exactly what another distrusts.

Coverage

One agent sees what another structurally cannot. Not a conflict. A reach into a neighbour's blind field.

Shared silence

What no perspective addressed at all. Surfaced instead of papered over.

Fig. 06What stops being possible

Problems the substitution _removes

These are not mitigations. Each failure below is produced by the merge operation itself, so removing the operation removes the failure class.

  • Consensus collapse

    Agents converge on agreeable output because the pipeline rewards agreement.removed · no merge exists; the map requires typed difference to be well formed

  • Decision-space capture

    The aggregation layer pre-selects which options ever reach the person.removed · every output arrives intact; nothing upstream filters the option set

  • Accountability diffusion

    “The system recommended it,” with no attributable position behind the verdict.removed · every claim carries its agent; a named human closes the run

  • Error cascades

    One wrong early merge propagates through every downstream step.removed · no step consumes a merged artifact; errors stay local and attributable

  • Hidden weighting

    The synthesis logic is buried in prompts and aggregation code nobody can audit.removed · weights are explicit, user-set, computed in the open on the client

  • False confidence

    A fluent answer hides variance; gaps in coverage are invisible in smooth prose.removed · shared silence is a first-class output

  • Deskilling

    The person consumes verdicts while their own capacity for synthesis atrophies.removed · the person performs the synthesis; the tool exercises the capacity it serves

An orchestration pattern either grows its user's ability to hold complexity, or it rents that ability out and bills for it monthly.

Fig. 07What it costs

The honest _page

The pattern is expensive and I will not pretend otherwise. Five parallel model calls plus a cartography pass cost several times what one call costs, in tokens and in latency. The output demands more of the reader. A conflict map takes longer to work through than a verdict, and most people arriving at a decision want relief, not a structured account of their uncertainty.

So the scope boundary matters. Deterministic tasks, tasks with a checkable answer, high-volume automation: converge, merge, ship. Synarchic orchestration earns its cost only where the answer is contestable and the stakes justify deliberation. The rule of thumb: if nobody could reasonably disagree with the output, you did not need this architecture. If somebody could, you needed to see the disagreement.

One more cost. A synarchic system is still a configured epistemic space. I chose which perspectives sit on the council, I wrote their founding oppositions, I decided that visible conflict is a virtue. That is a framing, and a framing is a mild form of the capture this essay argues against. The architecture's answer is to make its own configuration editable. Agents can be added, removed, and rewritten by the person who uses them. Sovereignty over a council you did not appoint is not sovereignty.

The critique has to apply to the critic's own system, or it is marketing.

Fig. 08The acceptance criterion

One question, asked of any _pipeline

Shmakov's framework reduces to a single test, and the test survives translation out of his vocabulary entirely. After the run completes, does the person hold more of the decision or less?

A merge-terminated system answers less, every time, by design. A synarchic system passes when its user needs it less over time for the same class of decision, and brings it harder ones instead. That is a measurable property. It belongs in the acceptance criteria next to latency and cost, and as agentic systems take over more of what organisations call thinking, it may turn out to be the only metric that was ever about the human at all.
